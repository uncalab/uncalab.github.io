Report: the Academy of Aphasia 2026

The hottest topics of the meeting and our reflections.

From 4 to 6 October 2026, the University of Liège hosted the 64th Annual Meeting of the Academy of Aphasia, one of the leading international conferences devoted to the study of the neural foundations of language and language disorders.

The conference reflected a clear shift in aphasia research. Traditional clinical classifications remain useful, but the field is moving towards more individualized characterization. In addition to lesion type and location, increasing attention is being paid to the white-matter pathways affected, communication between the hemispheres, preserved learning mechanisms, and disrupted neurochemical systems.

Rethinking the neural organization of language

One of the main debates concerned whether there is a brain network specialized for language. Presentations by Idan A. Blank, Leonardo Fernandino, Peter Hagoort, and Elizabeth Jefferies showed that certain brain regions are particularly sensitive to linguistic processing, but that language comprehension and production also require systems involved in conceptual knowledge, memory, action, and cognitive control.

Other studies shifted the focus from lesion location to brain disconnection. Findings presented by Mihailo Avramov linked different white-matter pathways to word and sentence comprehension. Michel Thiebaut de Schotten’s keynote address broadened this perspective through the study of the connectome and brain bioenergetics.

New approaches to characterizing aphasia were also presented. NeuroT-Map, used by Pedro Nascimento Alves and colleagues, estimates which neurotransmitter systems are affected by a lesion. Other studies identified anatomical profiles that did not map directly onto the classical aphasia subtypes and showed that interhemispheric relationships cannot be reduced to simple competition between the two hemispheres.

Artificial intelligence applied to aphasia

Artificial intelligence was one of the central themes of the conference. Applications included automatic speech recognition, natural language processing, machine learning, and large language models.

Current systems can extract discourse measures, support aphasia screening, analyse the amount and organization of information, and identify patterns associated with severity or clinical profile. A brief connected-speech sample detected acute aphasia with a balanced accuracy of 86%, highlighting the potential of discourse analysis to support initial assessment.

The main obstacle remains the automatic transcription of aphasic speech. Paraphasias, pauses, repetitions, omissions, and motor speech impairments increase system errors and may cause clinically relevant information to be lost. It is therefore essential to distinguish between automatically analysing a human-reviewed transcript and processing an audio recording directly without supervision.

Models were also presented that can analyse temporal coherence, estimate the relevant information conveyed in a narrative, characterize bilingual responses, or suggest words to people experiencing word-finding difficulties. These applications offer a path from language analysis towards real-time communication support, although they remain less developed than tools for assessment and characterization.

Assessment, treatment, and clinical practice

The conference included proposals to shorten assessment time, develop instruments for languages with limited resources, and facilitate intensive or remote treatment. These included syntactic interventions, bilingual therapy delivered by videoconference, and remotely supervised neuromodulation procedures.

Another important message was that personalization cannot be based solely on tests, neuroimaging, or algorithms. People with aphasia placed particular emphasis on communication and participation, whereas family members assigned greater importance to emotional, social, and economic consequences. Treatment goals should therefore be agreed with the person with aphasia and should incorporate the family’s perspective without replacing the individual’s own voice.

Remaining challenges

Several steps are still required before artificial intelligence can be translated into clinical practice:

  • Improve automatic recognition of aphasic speech.
  • Evaluate models according to aphasia severity, fluency, motor speech impairments, and language.
  • Validate models across different centres and populations.
  • Compare their usefulness with professional assessment.
  • Demonstrate that they reduce assessment time, detect meaningful changes, or improve clinical decisions and communication outcomes.
  • Maintain human oversight at clinically relevant stages.

Taken together, the studies presented point towards an aphasiology that is more personalized, computational, and responsive to linguistic diversity. The aim should not be to replace professional assessment, but to complement it with tools that are more accurate, reproducible, and responsive to individual needs.

The Cognitive Neuroscience and Aphasia Unit at the University of Málaga participated with research examining the effects of combining donepezil with intensive language therapy on reading, as well as the characterization of echolalia in post-stroke aphasia.

Written by María José Torres Prioris
